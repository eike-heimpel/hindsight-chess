# Positioning

> One concept per file; they interlock but evolve independently.

## The landscape (and they're free)

- **chess.com Game Review** — the incumbent. Built into the app 100M+ people
  already use. Win%, classifications, accuracy, and now narrated "coach"
  commentary. This is the gravity well we orbit.
- **Lichess** — free, open-source, world-class engine analysis, "learn from your
  mistakes."
- **DecodeChess** — natural-language explanation of positions as its whole
  product; closest competitor to our explainer.
- **Aimchess** — cross-game stats, weakness detection, training plans.

## The brutal truth

We **cannot out-engine the engine**, and engine analysis is a commodity that
chess.com gives away to retain players. Any product whose value is "eval +
classifications + accuracy + explain-a-move" has no answer to "why not just use
the free Game Review in the app where I already play?" We must win on a layer the
incumbent is structurally bad at or uninterested in.

## The moat (three layers, thin individually, strong stacked)

1. **Human-relevant, not engine-perfect.** We grade you against a _better you_,
   not Stockfish — surfacing only mistakes a person could realistically have
   found, filtering engine-only spikes. This is taste, not tech, and it's the
   hardest thing on this list to copy.
2. **Stats-interface craft.** Eike's edge (ex-LEC analyst + overlay PM): making
   dense game-state legible and _emotionally engaging_ for someone consuming it.
   Most chess stats UIs are spreadsheets. Ours is the front door people love.
3. **The post-game home habit.** Owning the moment after every game — a place you
   return to — is a behavioral moat the incumbents leave on the table because
   their job is to keep you _playing_, not to graduate you.

Cross-platform ownership (chess.com **and** lichess, your data is yours) is a
fourth lever we can pull if we want breadth as a differentiator.

## Why not chess.com?

Because Game Review grades you against a machine, forgets you the moment you close
it, and is built to keep you playing — not to make you _feel_ something about the
game you just finished or to be the place you come home to. We are the opposite of
all three.

## The acquisition angle

A chess.com-style acquisition is a real, good upside. But be clear about _what_
would be acquirable: **not** the engine analysis (they have it). It's (a) a
beloved post-game experience that retains the segment their own review serves
blandly, (b) the human-relevant-mistake philosophy, and (c) the explainer voice
and stats craft. Building beginner _delight_ is therefore the same path as
building an acquirable asset — the goals don't fight.

## Defensibility — stay honest

The technical moat is thin; the incumbent _could_ build the "remembers you" layer.
Our durable defense is **taste + brand + habit** (and optionally cross-platform
ownership), not algorithms. That's a fine moat for a beloved niche product — it is
not a fortress. Plan accordingly: win on love, not on lock-in.
